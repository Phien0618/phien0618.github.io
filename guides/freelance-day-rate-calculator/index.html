Freelance day rate calculator

Most day rate calculators divide your target income by about 220 days and stop. The answer comes out too low because it ignores two things: the days you won't work at all (holidays, sick days, public holidays), and the days you'll work but can't bill (sales, admin, invoicing, learning). This calculator handles both, then gives you a day rate, a half-day rate, an hourly rate and a weekly rate you can quote. The formula is shown underneath, so you can check it yourself.

The calculator

The formula

  1. Revenue needed = (target income + business costs) × (1 + buffer %)
  2. Working days = 260 weekdays − holidays − public holidays − sick days
  3. Billable days = working days × (1 − non-billable %)
  4. Day rate = revenue needed ÷ billable days

With the default numbers: (60,000 + 6,000) × 1.10 = 72,600 needed. 260 − 25 − 8 − 5 = 222 working days. 222 × 0.75 = 166.5 billable days. 72,600 ÷ 166.5 = 436.04 a day, or about 54.50 an hour over an 8-hour day. The same inputs with the naive "divide by 220" method give 330 a day, which is roughly 25% short. That shortfall is the unbilled time.

Why the non-billable share matters most

Of all the inputs, the non-billable percentage moves the result the most, and it's the one people guess most optimistically. Think about an ordinary month. How many days went to finding work (proposals, calls, portfolio updates), running the business (invoices, chasing payments, bookkeeping, email) and getting better (courses, tool setup)? None of that is billable, and all of it is necessary.

A quick way to estimate it: track one normal month in a simple spreadsheet, one row per day, marking each half-day as billable or not. If you can't track yet, start at 25–35% and adjust once you have real numbers. Newer freelancers with an empty pipeline usually sit at the high end, because more of their time goes to finding clients.

Even if you never quote a day rate to a client, you need to know it. A project price is just your day rate times your estimated days, plus contingency. The project quoting guide walks through that step by step.

Half-day rates: don't just halve it

A half-day booking rarely leaves you with a usable second half. There's context switching, travel or call prep, and it's hard to sell the other half to someone else. A common approach is to set the half-day rate at around 60% of the day rate. The calculator above uses 60%. Change it in your own pricing if your work splits cleanly.

Sanity-check against the market

The calculator tells you what you need to charge, not what clients in your niche will pay. Check the result against public evidence: rates published by freelancers in your field, rate surveys from your industry association, or day rates in contract job ads for similar work. This page doesn't quote market rates, because they vary too much by country, skill and niche to be useful here.

  • If your needed rate is below the market: charge the market rate. Your costs set a floor, not a ceiling.
  • If it's above the market: lower costs, raise utilisation (by fixing your sales pipeline), specialise in a niche that pays more, or package work into fixed-price projects where speed earns you more.

How to state your day rate to a client

State it plainly, with what's included, and don't apologise for it.

My day rate is [440], which covers a working day of up to [7.5] hours, including [prep / a short summary at the end of the day]. Half-days are [265]. For this project I'd estimate [6] days, so [2,640], invoiced [50% up front and 50% on delivery]. Happy to talk through the estimate if useful.

Round to a clean number (440, not 436.04). Odd numbers make the rate look negotiable. When you put your rate up later, use the rate increase email templates.

Common mistakes

  • Using your old salary as the target without adding costs. An employer also paid for your equipment, software, holidays and pension. Now you do.
  • Assuming 100% billable time. Nobody bills every working day, so plan for the gaps.
  • Forgetting tax timing. The target above is before tax, so set your tax aside as invoices are paid. How much depends on your country and setup, so check with an accountant.
  • Never recalculating. Run the numbers again every year, or whenever your costs or utilisation change.
