What should a freelance rate increase email say, and when do I send it for January 1?

If your new rate starts on January 1 and you want to give 60 days' notice, the email has to go out by November 2. Below: the timeline, one full rate increase email template to copy, and what to reply when a client pushes back.

The timeline: send date → effective date

Notice you giveSend the email byNew rate from
60 daysNovember 2January 1
45 daysNovember 17January 1
30 days (minimum)December 2January 1

Why 60 days for recurring and retainer clients? Many of them set next year's budget in November and December. An email that lands before that lets them plan for your new rate, and gives them time to book work at the current rate if they want to. Thirty days is the shortest notice to give anyone; for a client who pays you every month, aim for more.

What the email needs (5 parts)

  1. The new rate and the date, in the first two sentences. Give the current rate too, so nobody has to look it up.
  2. What stays the same: work in progress and anything booked before the date keeps the current rate.
  3. A thank-you that is about the relationship, not an apology for the increase.
  4. One easy next step, such as booking work before the change.
  5. No long justification. One line is fine; a page of reasons invites negotiation.

Freelance rate increase email template (full sample)

This is Email A from the Freelance Pricing & Quote Kit ("existing client, with notice"), with the bracketed dates set to January 1. Replace everything in [brackets].

Subject: My rates from [January 1] Hi [Name], I'm writing to let you know about a change to my rates. From [January 1], my hourly rate will be [100] (currently [90]), and my project packages will go up in line with it. Any project already in progress, and anything you book before [January 1], stays at the current rate. Thank you for working with me over the past [year]. If you'd like to book work before the change, reply to this email and I'll hold the dates. Best, [Your name]

On a monthly retainer? Name the fee instead of the hourly rate, keep the deliverables listed, and offer a smaller package at the old fee as the alternative (the kit's Email C does exactly this).

What to do if they push back

Once the email is sent, don't negotiate the rate itself. Change the scope, the timing or the package instead. Three replies from the kit (bracketed dates adjusted for a January 1 start):

"That's more than we budgeted for."

Understood. Two options: book the next project before [January 1] at the current rate, or we adjust the scope so it fits your budget at the new rate. I'm happy to suggest what to trim.

"Can you keep our old rate?"

I value working with you, which is why I'm giving you notice and keeping your current rate until [date]. After that, the new rate applies to all my clients, so it stays fair for everyone.

"Can you phase it in?"

Yes. The rate goes to [95] from [January 1] and to [100] from [April 1]. Both dates are fixed, so you can budget for them now.

If there's no reply after a week, send one short check-in that the email arrived. Silence is not a no, and you don't need their permission for the new rate to start.

What is the raise worth if a client leaves?

This is the question that stops most freelancers from sending the email. The arithmetic is simpler than it feels. Take the kit's worked example: a designer going from 90 to 100 per hour with 88 billable hours a month.

  • Today: 90 × 88 = 7,920 a month.
  • Break-even client loss: 1 − 90 ÷ 100 = 10%. Lose less than 10% of your client hours and you earn more than before.
  • Losing 5% of hours: 100 × 83.6 = 8,360 a month (+440).

And the worse case? The kit's offline calculator (Step 5) runs a 12-month stress test: lose 15% of client hours, more than break-even, and refill them over 4 months at the new rate. The running total dips to −550 in month 2, turns positive in month 4 and ends at +7,260 after 12 months. That tells you the cash cushion to keep (550 here) before you hit send.

These are example numbers, not a prediction. Put in your own rate, hours and a loss you think is realistic. The calculator also shows the worst case with no refill at all (−5,280 over 12 months here), so you can decide with both ends in view. Planning tool, not financial advice.

Your October checklist

  1. Pick the new rate and the date (January 1).
  2. Run your numbers: break-even loss and the cushion you need.
  3. Decide per client: everyone at once, new clients first (current clients keep the old rate until a later date), or a phased increase.
  4. Send the emails by November 2, one per client, not a group email.
  5. Update your quote template and rate card for January.
