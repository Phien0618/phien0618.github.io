How to tell existing clients you're raising your rates (5 email templates)

Raising rates with clients you already have is harder than quoting a new one. You know them, they know your old number, and the email feels like a confrontation. It doesn't need to be. A good rate-increase email is short, gives a clear date, doesn't apologize, and leaves the client with an easy next step.

Below you'll find a calculator that shows how much a raise is actually worth, a short checklist, and five emails you can copy for the most common situations: hourly clients, monthly retainers, project-based work, a softer "grandfathered" version, and a reply for when a client pushes back.

First: what is the raise actually worth?

A lot of freelancers put off raising rates because they're afraid of losing a client. Run the numbers first. This calculator shows your income before and after the increase, and how much client work you could lose before you'd end up earning less.

The arithmetic: at a new rate of r × (1 + p), you earn the same with 1/(1 + p) of the work. With a 15% raise, that means you could lose about 13% of your billable work and come out even.

Before you send anything: a 6-point checklist

  1. Pick a number you can defend. For an existing client, 10–20% at a time is common and easy to accept. If you're far below market, think about a bigger jump for new clients and step existing ones up over two rounds.
  2. Read your contract. If it fixes rates for a term or requires a notice period, follow it. A rate increase can't override a signed agreement. It applies from the next term or renewal.
  3. Give real notice. 30 days is the minimum. 60 days is kinder for retainers and for clients who set budgets quarterly. Always name an exact date, not "next month".
  4. Time it well. Good moments: right after a successful delivery, at contract renewal, or ahead of a new calendar or fiscal year. Bad moments: in the middle of a project, or the week after you missed a deadline.
  5. Send it as a separate email. If you bury it in an invoice or a status update, the client may feel ambushed later.
  6. Decide your walk-away point in advance. Know whether you'd keep the client at the old rate, meet halfway, or let them go. Then you won't be negotiating against yourself on a call.

Template 1: hourly or daily-rate client

Subject: Rate update effective [DATE] Hi [NAME], Thanks for another good stretch of work together. [ONE SPECIFIC RESULT, e.g. "The new onboarding flow went live on schedule and support tickets have dropped."] I'm writing to let you know that from [DATE, at least 30 days away] my rate will be [NEW RATE] per [hour/day], up from [OLD RATE]. Anything we start before that date will be billed at the current rate. I'm looking forward to continuing the work on [UPCOMING PROJECT/AREA]. If you'd like to talk about how this affects your budget or planning, just reply and we'll find a time. Best, [YOUR NAME]

Why it works: it opens with a concrete result rather than a vague thank-you, states the change once with both numbers and a date, protects work already in progress, and offers a conversation without asking for permission.

Template 2: monthly retainer

Subject: Retainer update for [MONTH YEAR] Hi [NAME], Over the past [PERIOD] the retainer has covered [WHAT YOU ACTUALLY DO, e.g. "weekly content, the monthly report and ad-hoc design requests"]. I've enjoyed it and I'd like to keep it going. Starting with the [MONTH] invoice, the retainer will be [NEW AMOUNT]/month (currently [OLD AMOUNT]). Scope stays the same: [SHORT SCOPE LIST]. If the budget is tight, an alternative is to keep the current fee and reduce the scope to [SMALLER SCOPE]. Let me know which works better by [DATE], and I'll update the agreement. Thanks, [YOUR NAME]

Why it works: retainer clients care most about predictability. Offering a smaller-scope option at the old price gives them a "yes" either way. Both outcomes raise your effective rate.

Template 3: project-based client

Subject: Pricing for upcoming projects Hi [NAME], Now that [LAST PROJECT] is wrapped, a quick heads-up for planning: from [DATE] my project pricing is changing. A project like [LAST PROJECT] would now be quoted at around [NEW PRICE] (it was [OLD PRICE]). Anything you send me before [DATE] I'll still quote at the current pricing. Happy to look at what's coming up next whenever you're ready. Best, [YOUR NAME]

Why it works: project clients don't buy hours, so frame the change around a project they recognize. The "quote before [DATE]" line is a fair deadline that often brings in work early.

Template 4: the gentle version (grandfathering a long-term client)

Subject: A note about my rates Hi [NAME], You were one of my first clients, and I really value that. My rate for new clients is now [NEW RATE]. I've kept yours at [OLD RATE] until now, and I'd like to move it to [MIDPOINT RATE] from [DATE, 60 days away]. It will stay there through at least [MONTH YEAR]. Thanks for the trust you've put in me. I'm glad to answer any questions. Warmly, [YOUR NAME]

Why it works: it's honest that they already have a discount, the step up is smaller, and the guaranteed period gives them certainty. Use this for clients you'd really hate to lose.

Template 5: replying when a client pushes back

Hi [NAME], Thanks for being upfront, I appreciate it. I understand the new rate is more than you'd planned for. The rate itself is staying at [NEW RATE], but a few options might help: - Reduce the scope to [SMALLER SCOPE] so the monthly total stays close to what you pay now. - Move the start date of the new rate to [LATER DATE] so it falls in your next budget cycle. - [If true] A [3/6]-month commitment at [SLIGHTLY LOWER RATE]. If none of these work, I understand, and I'll make sure everything is handed over cleanly. Just let me know which way you'd like to go. Best, [YOUR NAME]

Why it works: you hold the rate but stay flexible on scope, timing and commitment. Offering a clean handover shows you're not bluffing, and that alone often settles things.

Mistakes that make clients say no

  • Apologizing or over-explaining. Saying "I'm so sorry, costs have gone up and I hate to do this…" invites negotiation. One sentence of context is enough.
  • Leaving out the old number. Clients will look it up anyway. Show both so there are no surprises on the invoice.
  • Using percentages only. "A 15% increase" makes them do math. Give the actual new figure.
  • No date, or a date that's too soon. Without a clear effective date the change feels optional. A date that's too soon feels hostile.
  • Raising everyone at once without a plan. Stagger it if a few clients make up most of your income. Use the calculator above to see the risk.

After you send it

Most clients reply with a short "noted, thanks", or don't reply at all. Treat silence as acceptance, but mention the new rate again in the last invoice before the change ("Reminder: from [DATE], the rate is [NEW RATE]"). Update your proposal template and contracts on the same day so new clients get quoted the new number right away.
